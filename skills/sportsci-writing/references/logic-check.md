# Logic Check for Sport Science Manuscripts

Use this reference when the user asks to check logic, argumentation, scientific rigor, internal consistency, overclaiming, or the relationship between the research question, methods, results, discussion, and conclusion.

Do not begin by polishing sentences. Audit the reasoning first. Preserve the author's intended meaning, but do not preserve an inference that the evidence cannot support.

## Core principle

Every major claim must be supported by at least one of the following:

- data reported in the study
- an appropriate statistical result
- an accurately cited source
- a clearly identified theoretical interpretation

If support is missing, label the claim as unsupported or uncertain. Do not invent evidence, citations, mechanisms, analyses, or author intentions.

## 1. Research question and rationale

Check whether:

- the background narrows to a specific research problem
- the stated research gap follows from the literature reviewed
- the objective directly addresses that gap
- each hypothesis identifies the relevant variables and expected direction
- the population, exposure or intervention, comparison, and outcome are sufficiently clear
- the objective remains consistent across the abstract and main text

Flag broad objectives such as "to explore the effects of exercise" when the population, intervention, comparator, outcome, or time frame is not specified.

## 2. Design and causal language

Match the strength of the wording to the study design:

- Cross-sectional studies support association, not temporal order or causation.
- Longitudinal observational studies may support temporal association but do not automatically establish causation.
- Non-randomized interventions remain vulnerable to confounding, selection, and regression to the mean.
- Randomized trials may support causal interpretation when allocation, adherence, missing data, contamination, and analysis are adequately addressed.
- Qualitative studies support interpretation of experiences and processes, not prevalence or effect estimates.
- Measurement or reliability studies support agreement or repeatability, not intervention effectiveness.

Flag causal verbs such as "caused", "improved", "led to", "resulted in", "促进", "导致", and "产生影响" when the design does not support them.

## 3. Methods and statistical alignment

Check whether:

- every primary and secondary outcome is defined and measured in Methods
- every reported result has a corresponding planned or clearly labelled exploratory analysis
- repeated observations, clusters, teams, classes, or matched data are handled appropriately
- covariates are defined, temporally appropriate, and justified
- the statistical model matches the outcome type and study design
- assumptions, missing data, exclusions, and multiplicity are handled or acknowledged
- effect sizes and confidence intervals are reported where appropriate
- subgroup, mediation, moderation, and sensitivity analyses are identified as planned or exploratory

Do not recommend a statistical method merely because it is more complex. Explain why the method fits the design and what question it answers.

## 4. Results

Check whether:

- the Results answer the objectives in a coherent order
- sample sizes, group labels, time points, units, and denominators are consistent
- the narrative direction agrees with the numerical values, tables, and figures
- non-significant results are not described as effective, improved, or beneficial
- a significant within-group change is not used to claim a between-group effect
- absence of statistical significance is not treated as evidence of equivalence
- percentage changes use the correct denominator
- missing data, attrition, excluded participants, and analysis populations are accounted for
- secondary or exploratory findings are not presented as primary results

Separate statistical significance from effect magnitude, precision, practical relevance, and clinical relevance.

## 5. Discussion

For each major discussion claim, identify:

1. the result being interpreted
2. its agreement or disagreement with previous evidence
3. the proposed explanation or mechanism
4. plausible alternative explanations
5. the uncertainty or design limitation that constrains interpretation

Flag mechanisms presented as established facts when they were not measured. Do not allow citations to substitute for the study's own results. Distinguish a post hoc explanation from a prespecified hypothesis.

## 6. Limitations

Check whether each limitation explains its likely consequence. Avoid generic lists that do not affect interpretation.

Useful structure:

- source of bias or uncertainty
- likely direction or consequence, when defensible
- aspect of the conclusion affected
- whether the issue can be reduced in future research

Do not claim that a large sample removes confounding, that statistical adjustment proves causality, or that acknowledging a limitation neutralizes it.

## 7. Conclusion and generalization

Check whether:

- the conclusion directly reflects the primary results
- causal strength matches the design
- claims remain within the sampled population, setting, intervention, and follow-up period
- feasibility, acceptability, efficacy, and effectiveness are not conflated
- statistical significance is not confused with practical importance
- null findings are described with appropriate attention to precision
- limitations materially constrain the conclusion where necessary

## 8. Internal consistency

Compare the abstract, main text, tables, figures, supplement, and response letter for:

- sample size and analysis population
- intervention and follow-up duration
- variable names, scales, coding, and units
- reference and control groups
- effect direction and estimate
- P values and confidence intervals
- primary and secondary outcome labels
- model numbering and adjustment sets
- terminology for participants, exposures, and outcomes

Do not silently choose between conflicting values. Report the discrepancy and identify the likely source only when evidence is available.

## 9. Claim-evidence map

For a full manuscript or Discussion section, create a compact claim-evidence map when useful:

| Claim | Evidence used | Evidence type | Support level | Required action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Main claim | Result, table, or citation | Direct / indirect / theoretical | Supported / partial / unsupported | Keep / hedge / revise / remove |

Use the map to identify unsupported mechanisms, overgeneralization, duplicated claims, and conclusions that rely on secondary outcomes.

## Output format

Report findings before rewriting. Use the following order:

### Major logic issues

Issues that could change the interpretation, validity, or main conclusion.

### Moderate logic issues

Incomplete reasoning, unsupported explanations, unclear alignment, or important ambiguity.

### Minor issues

Terminology, ordering, local clarity, or presentation inconsistencies that do not change the main interpretation.

For each issue provide:

- Location
- Problem
- Why it matters
- Recommended correction
- Suggested wording, when enough evidence is available

Distinguish clearly between confirmed errors, likely problems, and questions that require information from the author. If no substantive logic problem is found, say so and identify any remaining uncertainty or missing evidence.

## Rewriting after the audit

Rewrite only after the logic audit unless the user explicitly asks for a direct rewrite. During rewriting:

- correct unsupported causal or directional language
- preserve all verified numerical values and citations
- mark missing evidence rather than filling the gap
- keep hypotheses, outcomes, and conclusions aligned
- retain uncertainty when the design or estimate is inconclusive
- apply the relevant language and AI-trace references only after the reasoning is sound
